For roughly three decades, researchers have used peroxidases as platforms for asking a basic question in heme enzymology: how much of an enzyme’s chemistry comes from the heme itself, and how much comes from the protein around it?

Peroxidases are useful for this work because their folds can support well-characterized high-valent heme intermediates, while their active sites can be modified in ways that remain structurally interpretable. That makes it possible to change one feature at a time and ask what the change actually does.

## From changing a ligand to redesigning an active site

Early engineering studies tested how the heme’s immediate coordination environment affects reactivity. Later designs went further, introducing features inspired by other enzyme families. These included P450-like proximal ligation, manganese-binding sites, heme–copper centers, and coupling between heme and iron–sulfur cofactors.

The point of these models was not simply to build a small copy of another enzyme. Each design offered a way to test a specific idea: whether a particular ligand could shift heme electronics, whether a second metal could support a new reaction, or whether a transplanted cofactor arrangement could reproduce a useful part of a more complex enzyme’s chemistry.

## The protein environment still matters

A recurring lesson is that reproducing the first coordination shell is only a starting point. The residues and water molecules around the metal site help position substrates, deliver protons, organize electron transfer, and control access to reactive intermediates. Those second-shell features can determine whether a structurally plausible design also shows the intended activity.

This helps explain why structural resemblance, spectroscopic similarity, mechanism, and function should be evaluated separately. A design can reproduce a cofactor geometry without following the same reaction pathway, and it can show activity without yet establishing that the native enzyme’s mechanism has been recreated.

## What a common scaffold makes possible

Using peroxidase scaffolds across several design problems gives researchers a shared context for comparing what transfers between enzyme families and what depends on a larger protein architecture. The value is not that every redesign becomes a functional replica. It is that the scaffold makes selected parts of complex heme chemistry experimentally accessible.

I’m putting together a retrospective on this work, from the enzymology and structural biology that established the field to the more recent efforts to build new metal sites in peroxidase proteins. The broad picture is that the heme provides a versatile chemical center, but the protein matrix determines how that chemistry is organized and used.
